Chapter 04 · Surface kinetics and nucleation

From Nuclei to a Connected Film

Films often develop through incubation, nucleation, island growth, and coalescence. This chapter introduces idealized growth modes and explains why continuity and percolation depend on material, surface, and measurement scale.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the sequence from incubation through nucleation and island coalescence.
  • Distinguish Volmer–Weber, Frank–van der Merwe, and Stranski–Krastanov as idealized limiting modes.
  • Explain why geometric coverage, electrical percolation, and a continuous functional film are distinct criteria.

Incubation and the first stable nuclei

After exposure begins, incoming species may first adsorb, react with surface groups, diffuse, or desorb. The interval before a detectable stable population forms is often called incubation, but its measured duration depends on the detection method and threshold. A surface can undergo chemical changes before any instrument reports a film, so 'no signal' does not necessarily mean 'no surface reaction.'

A nucleus is a cluster that is sufficiently stable to persist and grow under the conditions. Forming one can require a fluctuation that crosses a free-energy barrier; the barrier depends on cluster size, interfaces, supersaturation, and strain. Nucleation is therefore stochastic and sensitive to defects and local chemistry. Classical nucleation pictures are useful organizing models but can omit atomistic structure and heterogeneous pathways [r01].

Island growth and coalescence

Once stable nuclei exist, incoming material can enlarge islands while new nuclei continue to form. Island density and spacing reflect the competition among arrival, diffusion, capture, and desorption. As islands meet, coalescence reduces exposed substrate area, but boundaries can leave voids, seams, or defects. Coalescence itself can also drive restructuring as the total boundary and surface energy changes.

The path from separated islands to a connected morphology is not captured by nominal deposited thickness alone. Material can accumulate preferentially on existing islands, making local thickness greater than the area-averaged amount. Roughness, shadowing, substrate topography, and chemistry influence whether gaps close. Growth and stress evolution can further alter morphology [r01] [r06].

Three idealized growth modes

In Volmer–Weber growth, three-dimensional islands are favored from early stages, often associated with unfavorable wetting of the substrate. In Frank–van der Merwe growth, successive layers are favored, corresponding to complete wetting in the idealized thermodynamic picture. In Stranski–Krastanov growth, one or more wetting layers form before islanding becomes favorable, commonly because accumulated strain changes the balance. These labels summarize limiting patterns rather than complete microscopic mechanisms.

Actual films may combine features, transition with thickness, or depart from the simple classifications because of kinetics, reconstruction, alloying, defects, and changing strain. A label should therefore be tied to observations and conditions, not inferred solely from nominal materials or lattice mismatch. Thermodynamic wetting arguments and kinetic accessibility must be considered together [r01] [r07].

Continuity, percolation, and practical meaning

Geometric coverage asks what fraction of a projected area is occupied by material. Percolation asks whether connected paths span the region for a specified connectivity rule. Electrical conduction additionally depends on the resistance of necks and junctions, while a barrier layer may fail functionally through a single pinhole even when its average coverage is high. Thus 'continuous' must be defined relative to the property and scale of interest.

A percolation threshold is not a universal thickness: it depends on island shape, size distribution, spatial correlations, substrate, and the measurement's resolution. A conduction measurement can identify a connected electrical path but cannot prove a chemically pinhole-free barrier. Imaging, electrical tests, and depth-sensitive analysis answer complementary questions. Report the operational criterion and uncertainty rather than treating the first detected signal as a universal continuity point [r01].

Example

Illustrative coverage estimate: imagine 100 equal projected surface cells, of which 70 contain islands. Geometric coverage is 70/100 = 0.70, or 70%. This alone says nothing about whether the occupied cells form a path across the sample, whether gaps are pinholes through the film, or whether electrical junctions conduct. Those require separate connectivity and functional measurements.

Limitations

  • The three named growth modes are idealized descriptions; mixed and evolving behavior is common.
  • Percolation and continuity depend on operational definitions, morphology, and measurement scale; no universal critical thickness is implied.

Review questions

  1. Why can incubation time depend on the measurement method?
  2. What physical balance distinguishes the three idealized growth modes?
  3. Why is electrical percolation insufficient to certify a pinhole-free barrier?
