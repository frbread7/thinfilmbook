Chapter 06 · Deposition methods and transport

Chemical Vapor Deposition: Transport and Reaction

Builds a quantitative picture of precursor delivery, gas and surface reactions, transport limits, and wafer-scale nonuniformity in CVD.

Learning objectives

  • Trace precursor transport from inlet to reactive surface.
  • Distinguish surface-reaction-limited and mass-transfer-limited growth.
  • Use a series-resistance rate model only under its assumptions.
  • Explain how depletion, loading, and flow affect uniformity.

Delivery and the near-surface region

CVD supplies volatile reactants to a heated or otherwise activated surface, where chemical transformations produce a solid film and volatile by-products. Delivery includes inlet mixing, convection through the reactor, diffusion across the near-surface boundary layer, and adsorption or reaction at the surface. Temperature and composition can vary along this route, so an inlet partial pressure is not necessarily the concentration experienced by every point on a substrate [r05].

A boundary-layer description is useful when a relatively slow-moving near-wall region separates bulk flow from the surface. Reactant must cross this region to reach the film, while products must leave it. The layer is not a fixed geometrical coating: its thickness and concentration profile depend on flow, geometry, temperature, diffusivity, and surface consumption. Real reactors may require coupled fluid-flow, heat-transfer, and chemistry models rather than a single boundary-layer estimate.

Competing reaction and transport rates

A surface-reaction-limited regime occurs when transport can replenish reactant faster than the surface consumes it. The surface concentration then remains near its bulk value, and changes in surface kinetics, often strongly temperature-dependent, control growth. A mass-transfer-limited regime occurs when surface consumption is fast relative to replenishment; the near-surface reactant concentration falls and growth responds to transport. These are limiting descriptions, not mutually exclusive reactor labels: different locations or reactants can occupy different regimes at once.

For a steady, one-dimensional, single-reactant model with linear first-order surface consumption, constant diffusivity and transfer coefficient, no gas-phase reaction, and negligible product feedback, write the molar flux as J = k_g(C_b − C_s) = k_s C_s. Here k_g is a mass-transfer coefficient, k_s is an effective surface rate coefficient, C_b is bulk concentration, and C_s is surface concentration. Solving gives J = C_b/(1/k_g + 1/k_s), a series-resistance form. It is not a universal CVD rate law: nonlinear adsorption, multiple reactants, changing surface coverage, gas-phase chemistry, or transient conditions invalidate this simple expression [r05].

Loading, depletion, and uniformity

As reactive area or substrate count increases, the reactor presents a larger total sink for precursor. If supply and mixing do not scale accordingly, upstream surfaces can consume reactant and downstream surfaces see a depleted feed. This loading effect can shift growth rate, composition, or phase along a flow path. The result depends on reactor geometry, residence time, temperature field, reaction probability, and gas replenishment; the word loading by itself does not define a quantitative correction.

Uniformity engineering therefore considers the whole coupled reactor: inlet distribution, gas velocity, boundary-layer development, temperature gradients, pump and exhaust placement, and the spatial distribution of reactive area. A wafer may show radial variation even without strong inlet-to-outlet depletion, for example when temperature or flow varies across it. Thickness uniformity also does not guarantee uniform composition or defect density. Measurements should sample the outputs that matter, and a model should be checked against the specific reactor and operating domain before it is used for optimization.

Model use and interpretation

Dimensionless groups and transport calculations help identify plausible controlling mechanisms, but their values require appropriate properties and length scales. Gas diffusivity, viscosity, density, and reaction rates can vary with temperature and composition. A calculation using a single nominal value is best treated as a local estimate whose assumptions are stated. When these properties or boundary conditions are poorly known, the uncertainty can be comparable to the predicted process trend.

A useful modeling sequence is to state the geometry and steady-state assumptions, estimate transport and reaction time scales, and then ask whether coupling can be neglected. If a model predicts depletion, temperature gradients, or changing surface coverage, those effects should be represented or measured rather than folded into an unexplained empirical constant. Reactor design and optimization rely on transport models, but their credibility depends on boundary conditions and validation data for the relevant apparatus [r05].

Example

Illustrative only: under the stated linear model, let C_b = 1 mol m⁻³, k_g = 0.020 m s⁻¹, and k_s = 0.0050 m s⁻¹. Then 1/k_g = 50 s m⁻¹ and 1/k_s = 200 s m⁻¹, so J = 1/(250) = 0.0040 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. The surface concentration is C_s = J/k_s = 0.80 mol m⁻³. The lower surface coefficient limits the flux in this example. These chosen values are arithmetic inputs, not recommended process values.

Limitations

  • The series-resistance equation assumes steady, one-dimensional transport and linear first-order surface consumption of one reactant; it excludes gas-phase chemistry and coupled reactants.
  • No reactor-specific flow field, precursor properties, or process window is supplied; the chapter does not provide fab recipes or predict production uniformity.

Review questions

  1. What physical conditions distinguish surface-reaction-limited growth from mass-transfer-limited growth?
  2. Derive the series-resistance flux relation and state two assumptions whose failure would make it inappropriate.
  3. How can greater reactive loading create downstream depletion, and why might thickness mapping alone miss a uniformity problem?
