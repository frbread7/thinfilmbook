Chapter 10 · Interfaces, diffusion, and stress

Film Stress, Adhesion, and Delamination

Separates intrinsic and thermal stress, explains mismatch-driven loading and fracture pathways, and derives the Stoney stress relation with its assumptions and sign-convention limits.

Learning objectives

  • Separate intrinsic and thermal contributions conceptually and experimentally.
  • Relate thermal expansion mismatch to biaxial film stress under constrained strain.
  • Recognize adhesion, cohesive fracture, and delamination as distinct failure paths.
  • Apply the Stoney relation only when its thin-film and substrate assumptions are reasonable.

Where film stress comes from

Intrinsic stress develops during growth through mechanisms such as island coalescence, defect incorporation, grain-boundary evolution, and atomic peening. Its sign and magnitude depend on material, microstructure, and process history; a single mechanism rarely explains every thickness regime. Thermal stress arises when film and substrate would freely expand or contract by different amounts during a temperature change but remain bonded. Measurements after deposition generally include a history-dependent combination of these contributions, plus possible curvature or instrument artifacts. [r06]

For an isotropic film constrained in-plane by a much stiffer substrate, a simple elastic estimate is σ_th≈M_f(α_s−α_f)ΔT, where M_f=E_f/(1−ν_f) is the film biaxial modulus and α_s, α_f are the substrate and film expansion coefficients. This relation assumes uniform temperature, linear elastic response, a suitable reference state, and no relaxation, plasticity, cracking, or interfacial slip. It predicts a component, not the complete measured stress.

From stress to fracture and delamination

A film can fail within its own material, at an interface, or through a coupled crack path. Adhesion describes resistance to separation at an interface, while cohesive strength describes resistance to fracture within a material. Residual stress stores elastic energy; when a crack or debonding path can release enough energy, a film may buckle or delaminate. The outcome depends on stress, elastic properties, thickness, flaw geometry, interface toughness, and boundary conditions, not on stress alone. [r06]

An apparent adhesion value is meaningful only with a defined test geometry and failure mode. Scratch, blister, pull-off, and four-point bend methods load the interface differently and can instead fail in an adjacent layer or fixture. Report the specimen stack, preparation, loading protocol, observed fracture surface, and uncertainty. A process change that improves one interface metric may introduce stress, roughness, or chemical changes elsewhere in the stack.

Curvature and the Stoney relation

For a film much thinner than a thick, isotropic substrate, the classic Stoney relation estimates average equibiaxial film stress from a curvature change: σ_f≈(M_s t_s²/(6t_f))Δκ, with substrate biaxial modulus M_s=E_s/(1−ν_s), substrate thickness t_s, film thickness t_f, and curvature change Δκ relative to a defined reference. The derivation treats the film as a uniform thin layer, assumes small curvature change and linear elastic substrate behavior, and neglects details such as substrate anisotropy, multilayer coupling, and strong stress gradients. [r08]

The formula gives a signed stress only after the curvature convention, sample orientation, and before/after definition are fixed. Different instruments and software may define positive curvature oppositely, so a reported sign without convention is ambiguous. For nonuniform films, thick coatings, anisotropic substrates, large deflection, or multilayers, use a model appropriate to the geometry and report that model rather than presenting the Stoney estimate as exact.

Interpretation and reporting

Curvature during deposition can track stress evolution, while ex situ measurements provide a net state after cool-down and handling. A thermal cycle can reveal reversible mismatch response separately from irreversible relaxation only when temperature, reference curvature, and instrumental drift are controlled. Comparing thickness series and deposition stages can help identify transitions, but correlation with a growth mechanism is not proof of that mechanism. [r06]

A defensible report states the measured quantity, sign convention, calibration, layer and substrate properties used, reference temperature or curvature, and uncertainty. Distinguish measured curvature from inferred stress and identify all assumptions in the conversion. When interface integrity matters, pair stress measurements with a failure-mode observation or a relevant adhesion test; do not treat low average stress as evidence of strong adhesion.

Worked example

Illustrative values only: take E_s=130 GPa, ν_s=0.28, t_s=500 μm, t_f=1 μm, and measured Δκ=0.01 m⁻¹. Then M_s=130/(1−0.28)≈181 GPa and σ_f≈[181×10⁹ Pa×(500×10⁻⁶ m)²/(6×1×10⁻⁶ m)]×0.01 m⁻¹≈7.5×10⁷ Pa, or about 75 MPa in magnitude. The sign depends on the stated curvature convention. These illustrative inputs do not describe a validated material or process; the estimate assumes a thin uniform film and thick isotropic elastic substrate.

Limitations

  • Intrinsic and thermal stress are coupled in post-deposition measurements unless the thermal and growth histories are separated.
  • The Stoney relation is an approximation with restrictive geometry, elasticity, and uniformity assumptions.
  • Stress alone does not determine adhesion or predict delamination without fracture and flaw information.

Review questions

  1. Name two growth mechanisms that can contribute to intrinsic stress and one reason measured stress may also include thermal contribution.
  2. What assumptions and sign-convention details must accompany a Stoney-based stress value?
  3. How do interfacial adhesion failure and cohesive fracture differ, and why can low average stress fail to guarantee adhesion?
