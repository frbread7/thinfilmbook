Chapter 08 · Epitaxy and crystalline accommodation

Epitaxy: Registry, Strain, and Defects

Introduces crystal registry across an interface, lattice-mismatch strain, and the limits of critical-thickness descriptions for misfit-dislocation formation.

Learning objectives

  • Define epitaxial registry and distinguish orientation from lattice matching.
  • Calculate signed lattice mismatch using the stated convention.
  • Explain coherent strain and the energy tradeoff that can favor misfit dislocations.
  • Describe why critical-thickness predictions depend on model and growth conditions.

Crystal registry at an interface

Epitaxy describes film growth in which the crystal orientation of the deposited layer has a defined relationship to the underlying crystalline substrate. Registry concerns how lattice directions and planes align across the interface; it is distinct from merely having a preferred out-of-plane texture. Epitaxial alignment can occur even when the film and substrate lattice spacings differ, and multiple in-plane registries may be possible depending on symmetry and surface structure [r06].

The interface is not described by orientation alone. Chemical termination, reconstruction, intermixing, defects, and growth kinetics determine whether a proposed registry is realized and how it propagates. A measured orientation relationship therefore supports an epitaxy claim but does not by itself establish a defect-free interface, coherent strain, or a particular electrical quality. Structural and compositional evidence should be interpreted together with the growth context.

Mismatch and coherent strain

For a chosen in-plane lattice parameter convention, define signed mismatch as f = (a_f − a_s)/a_s, where a_f is the relaxed film lattice parameter and a_s is the substrate parameter along the compared direction. The sign states which lattice is larger under this convention; mismatch magnitude alone does not specify how strain is accommodated. For non-cubic materials, anisotropic surfaces, or domain-matching arrangements, the relevant in-plane periodicities must be defined explicitly.

A sufficiently thin film may remain coherent: its in-plane lattice is constrained to the substrate registry, and it stores elastic strain. In a simple small-strain picture, the film's in-plane strain is approximately (a_s − a_f)/a_f, which has the opposite sign convention and a slightly different denominator from f. Out-of-plane relaxation and elastic energy depend on the film's elastic constants and boundary conditions. Coherency is an interfacial state, not a guarantee of low total defect density or mechanical stability during later processing [r06].

Misfit dislocations and critical thickness

As a coherent layer thickens, the elastic energy stored by mismatch generally increases with film thickness. Introducing a misfit dislocation can reduce some of that elastic energy while costing line and core energy. This competition motivates a critical-thickness concept: beyond a model-dependent thickness, a relaxed state with dislocations may become energetically favorable. The dislocation's Burgers vector, line direction, interfacial geometry, elastic anisotropy, and any pre-existing defects affect the balance.

Classical critical-thickness models, including the Matthews-Blakeslee force-balance treatment, provide useful scaling arguments for selected idealized epitaxial systems [r07]. They do not give a universal threshold that a real film must obey. Predictions depend on definitions of mismatch and geometry, assumptions about dislocation character and equilibrium, and material elastic properties. Actual dislocations may nucleate at steps, edges, defects, or interfaces and may be kinetically delayed; a film can also relax by mechanisms outside the model. Treat predicted critical thickness as a model estimate with stated assumptions, not a fabrication limit.

Orientation, defects, and evidence

Orientation relationships influence which mismatch components and defect arrangements are available, but orientation does not uniquely determine strain relaxation. Domains, twins, stacking faults, threading dislocations, and interfacial misfit dislocations can coexist. Their populations are affected by surface preparation, nucleation, growth temperature, deposition rate, thickness, and later thermal or mechanical history. Thin-film defect formation is thus an evolution problem rather than a direct function of one lattice-number comparison [r06].

Assessing epitaxy and relaxation requires complementary evidence. Diffraction can reveal orientation, lattice spacing, and strain signatures, while microscopy can resolve local interface structure and defects; each method samples different volumes and has its own interpretation limits. A peak position alone may be influenced by composition, temperature, calibration, and strain. Conclusions should state which crystallographic directions were compared, the mismatch convention, whether the film is inferred coherent or relaxed, and the evidence supporting that inference.

Example

Illustrative arithmetic: let the relaxed film parameter along the chosen direction be a_f = 0.404 nm and substrate parameter be a_s = 0.400 nm. With f = (a_f − a_s)/a_s, f = (0.004 nm)/(0.400 nm) = +0.010, or +1.0%. The positive sign means the relaxed film spacing is larger in this convention. This value alone does not predict coherent strain, dislocation onset, or critical thickness.

Limitations

  • The mismatch definition compares selected in-plane parameters and may not represent anisotropic, domain-matched, or reconstructed interfaces without further specification.
  • Critical-thickness models idealize geometry, dislocation energetics, and equilibration; their estimates are not universal thresholds or validated process limits.

Review questions

  1. How does epitaxial registry differ from preferred texture, and what does an orientation measurement fail to establish?
  2. Using the chapter's convention, calculate f for a_f = 0.510 nm and a_s = 0.500 nm. Interpret the sign.
  3. Explain the energy tradeoff behind misfit-dislocation formation and name two reasons a critical-thickness prediction may differ from observed relaxation.
