Chapter 03 · Surface kinetics and nucleation

Surface Kinetics during Deposition

Arrival flux, sticking, residence, desorption, and surface diffusion determine how incoming species become incorporated material. The chapter connects these processes qualitatively to temperature, pressure, and flux without proposing fab recipes.

Learning objectives

  • Define incident flux and effective sticking probability and use them in a surface balance.
  • Explain how residence time and surface diffusion affect capture and incorporation.
  • Predict qualitative kinetic consequences of changing temperature, pressure, or flux while stating assumptions.

Arrival flux and sticking

The incident flux F is the number of species reaching a unit surface area per unit time. The flux at the substrate can differ from a source emission rate because transport, collisions, geometry, and chemical reactions intervene. In a simple gas-kinetic picture, flux depends on number density and the velocity distribution; translating pressure into substrate flux requires knowing the gas composition and transport regime.

The sticking probability S is the fraction of incident species retained for a defined observation interval or incorporated under specified conditions. It may depend on coverage, species energy, surface termination, and available reaction partners. A first-arrival estimate of net retention is F S, but this is not necessarily the film growth rate: retained species may desorb, diffuse to edges, or leave as reaction products [r01].

Residence and desorption

Residence time describes how long an adsorbed species remains on a surface before leaving or changing chemical state. In an activated single-site approximation, τ ≈ τ₀ exp(E_des/k_BT). Raising temperature can shorten residence dramatically when desorption is activated, but the same temperature change may also enable reactions or diffusion. The prefactor and barrier are material- and state-dependent, so this expression should be used to reason about trends, not to assign an unmeasured value.

If the surface has a distribution of binding sites or coverage-dependent chemistry, adsorbates do not share a single residence time. Readsorption can also extend the apparent residence. In self-limiting reaction sequences, surface termination and reactant exposure influence whether sites are consumed, regenerated, or blocked; ALD chemistry therefore cannot be reduced to a generic sticking coefficient [r02] [r03].

Surface diffusion and capture

An adsorbed species can hop between surface sites before desorbing or reacting. A simple random-walk picture gives a diffusion length of order ℓ_D ≈ (D_s τ)^1/2, where D_s is a surface diffusivity and τ a relevant lifetime. The numerical prefactor depends on dimensionality and definitions. Longer diffusion lengths can increase the chance of reaching a step, defect, or stable nucleus, but they may also promote aggregation or spatial redistribution.

Diffusivity is often modeled as D_s = D₀ exp(−E_diff/k_BT). Temperature can therefore increase mobility while reducing residence through desorption. The competition between these effects matters: a mobile species that promptly leaves may contribute less than a moderately mobile species that survives long enough to be captured. The substrate's steps, defects, reconstruction, and chemical termination alter both migration and capture probabilities [r01].

Temperature, pressure, and flux together

Temperature changes several rates at once: desorption, diffusion, surface reaction, and sometimes bulk incorporation. Pressure can alter arrival rate and gas-phase collision or transport behavior; its effect is not universally monotonic because reactor geometry and chemistry matter. Increasing flux can raise coverage and the frequency of encounters, while also changing the ratio of arrival to relaxation times. These controls are coupled rather than independent knobs.

A useful analysis names the rate-limiting steps and compares their characteristic times. If arrival is fast relative to surface rearrangement, nonequilibrium structures may be trapped; if desorption dominates, net accumulation may be small. Such reasoning is qualitative until barriers, transport, and surface state are measured or modeled for the particular system. Reactor-scale transport may need to be considered separately from surface kinetics [r05].

Example

Illustrative time-scale comparison: assume a hypothetical adsorbate has D_s = 1 nm²/s and lifetime τ = 4 s. The order-of-magnitude diffusion length is ℓ_D ≈ (D_s τ)^1/2 = (4 nm²)^1/2 = 2 nm. This estimate assumes a simple random walk with constant diffusivity and lifetime; it is not a measured material value or process prediction.

Limitations

  • The kinetic expressions are simplified models; real surfaces can have multiple sites, reactions, and coverage-dependent rates.
  • No temperature, pressure, flux, or recipe window is recommended; trends must be established for the actual system.

Review questions

  1. Why is incident flux multiplied by sticking probability not always equal to growth rate?
  2. How can increasing temperature simultaneously help and hinder incorporation?
  3. What assumptions are needed to interpret the diffusion-length estimate?
